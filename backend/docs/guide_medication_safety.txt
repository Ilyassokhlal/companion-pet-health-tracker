Giving medicines to a pet safely
Medicines only work as intended when they are given the right way, stored properly and kept out of reach between doses. Never give an animal a human medicine without approval from a veterinarian.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Giving a dog a pill
A pill can be hidden in a piece of cheese, a little peanut butter or a pill pocket treat. Gently holding the dog's mouth closed until it swallows also helps.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Giving a cat a pill
Hold the cat's head so its nose points up towards the ceiling. With the other hand, open the mouth by pulling gently down on the lower jaw, then place the pill as far back on the tongue as you can. A pet piller, a handheld device with a rubber tip, does the same job at arm's length. Hiding a pill in food is less reliable in cats, and medicating the food can put a cat off eating altogether.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Liquid and topical medicines
For a liquid, tilt the head slightly up, put the tip of the syringe into the mouth near the back teeth, and squeeze gently so the liquid goes towards the back of the mouth. Topical medicines go onto the skin or coat, and for a dog they are applied at the top of the neck where the dog cannot lick them off. Ask the veterinarian to demonstrate eye drops and ear medicines.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Read the label first
Read the label before giving anything, because how a medicine is given changes how well it works. Some medicines work better with food and others need an empty stomach. Ear medicine must never be put in the eyes, nor eye medicine in the ears. Some liquids have to be shaken first, because the active ingredient settles at the bottom of the bottle.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Finishing the course
Antibiotics are given at set intervals to keep enough medicine in the bloodstream to work. Missing doses, or stopping before the course is finished, can mean the infection is not cleared, that treatment has to be extended, or that the bacteria become resistant to the original medicine.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - A missed dose
What to do about a missed dose depends on the medicine, because some have to be spaced evenly to stay effective. The veterinary clinic can say when to give the next one. If too much was given by accident, call the veterinarian straight away.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - If your pet vomits the dose
Whether to repeat a dose that was vomited also depends on the medicine. Some break down quickly, so a pet that vomits soon after may already have absorbed the drug, and a second dose could then be too much. Ask the veterinarian rather than repeating it.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Side effects
Every medicine can have side effects, and the veterinarian should say which ones to watch for. As a general rule, stop the medicine and call the clinic if side effects appear. They may want to examine the animal.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Client information sheets
Some veterinary medicines come with a client information sheet, a plain language handout covering what the medicine is for, how to give it, what side effects to look for and what to do about them. Insulins and veterinary NSAIDs have them because dosing, storage and side effects matter so much. Where there is no sheet, the package insert holds the same kind of information.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Storing medicines
Every medicine has a temperature range it should be kept in, and storing it too warm or too cold can make it less effective. Some need refrigeration, some room temperature, and some must be kept out of sunlight. The label says which.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Keeping medicines out of reach
Store medicines where an animal cannot get at them. Many pet medicines are flavoured to taste good, so a dog or cat may sniff out the container and eat the whole supply, and some animals will chew the container too. People medicines are just as much of a risk: around half of the calls to one pet poison helpline are about pets that have eaten human medicines, with NSAID painkillers such as ibuprofen and naproxen at the top of the list.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Keeping human and pet medicines apart
Store your own medicines separately from your pet's. Owners sometimes take their pet's medicine by mistake, especially when the two are kept side by side.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - If a pet gets into medicine
If an animal swallows medicine it should not have, call the veterinarian or an animal poison control centre. The centres may charge a consultation fee. Report the problem afterwards, working with the veterinarian, whether the animal ate medicine by accident or was given the wrong one.

Giving medicines to a pet safely - Throwing medicines away
Take unused medicines to a drug take back location where one exists. A few powerful medicines are on a flush list and should be flushed instead. Otherwise mix them, without crushing tablets or capsules, with something unpalatable such as cat litter or used coffee grounds, seal the mixture in a bag or container and put it in the household rubbish, because dogs do go through the bin.
