Caring for a senior dog or cat
Growing older is not a disease. Older animals are more likely to develop certain conditions, but some stay entirely well, and changes that do appear are usually worth investigating rather than accepting.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - When an animal counts as senior
Dogs are generally treated as senior from about seven or eight years old, and cats from about eight or nine. Definitions have shifted as animals live longer: with better nutrition, indoor living and veterinary care, cats are often now regarded as older at twelve to fourteen.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Veterinary visits
Senior dogs should see a veterinarian twice a year or more, with blood tests or X rays suggested to catch problems early. Senior cats should also go twice a year or more, often with regular blood tests for early signs of chronic illness. Checks every six months suit many geriatric cats, and one approach is a yearly examination with blood work and X rays plus a shorter check in between.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Do not blame everything on age
Older animals tend to be less active and playful, may sleep more, may gain or lose weight, and may struggle to reach favourite places. These changes are often gradual, which makes them easy to dismiss, but they can be signs of common diseases or of dental problems that can be treated.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Getting around
Arthritis becomes more likely with age, and an older animal may also have less control over its bladder and bowels. Box steps, ramps or pet stairs let an animal reach a favourite chair or windowsill safely, and firm footing such as carpet on the ramp prevents slipping.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Litter boxes for older cats
Putting a litter box on every floor keeps one within easy reach. Some older cats will climb into a normal box to urinate but not to defecate, and a box with very low sides solves that.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Warmth and rest
Older cats seek out warm places, so keep the bed or favourite resting spot out of draughts. Aim for warm rather than hot, since an animal that cannot move away quickly can be burned.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Grooming help
An older animal may groom itself less well and benefit from more brushing, which removes loose hair, stimulates the skin and keeps the coat in better condition. This matters most for longhaired cats, who become uncomfortably matted. A sudden drop in how much the animal grooms itself can itself signal a health problem.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Failing eyesight
Night lights help an older animal with poor vision find its way. For a blind cat, keep the environment as consistent as possible, including furniture and litter boxes, call the cat's name before approaching so it is not startled, and avoid picking it up unless necessary.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Failing hearing
Approach a deaf or hard of hearing cat from the front rather than from behind, so it is not startled, and keep it indoors where it is safe from cars and predators.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Food and water
Fresh water and good food matter at every age and especially in old age. Easy access to clean water is particularly important for senior cats, because kidney function often declines with age. A veterinarian can advise on a diet with the right nutrition and calories for an older animal.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Routine and company
Older animals value predictable days more than younger ones do, and cope less well with change. Some become more dependent and want more attention, while others prefer to be left alone, so keeping to a familiar routine while following the animal's own preference works best.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - Confusion and restlessness at night
An old cat that wanders, seems lost, fails to recognise familiar people or becomes restless and anxious may have cognitive dysfunction, the feline equivalent of dementia. Calling out at night is also relatively common in cats with an overactive thyroid or high blood pressure, and high blood pressure can detach the retina and cause blindness, which brings its own anxiety and confusion. Those are treatable conditions, so yowling at night is worth a veterinary visit rather than earplugs.

Caring for a senior dog or cat - How old is old?
A dog aged twelve is a senior by any measure, as dogs are generally treated as senior from about seven or eight. Size matters more than anything else in how fast a dog ages: small breeds average ten to fifteen years and sometimes pass eighteen, medium breeds ten to thirteen, and giant breeds have the shortest lives at eight to thirteen years. Giant breeds also mature later, reaching adulthood at around two years old rather than the thirteen to fifteen months usual in other dogs.
