Pet emergencies and first aid
An emergency is any sudden, serious problem that needs quick medical attention: severe injuries, burns, poisoning, a sudden new illness, or a chronic condition that worsens quickly. Calling a veterinarian right away is the first step in nearly every case.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Signs that need a vet immediately
Get veterinary help immediately for poisoning, severe pain, severe or uncontrolled bleeding, trouble breathing, eye injuries, and a suspected broken bone or a limb the animal cannot move.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Signs that need a vet immediately
Other problems that need treatment straight away include trauma, shock, severe burns, seizures that keep going, an abnormal heart rhythm, loss of consciousness, blocked urine flow, an organ protruding from the body, a suspected snake bite, heat stroke, an open wound exposing bone, and difficulty giving birth.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Signs that need a vet immediately
Breathing with the mouth open, or a bluish tinge to the gums or skin means the animal is severely short of oxygen. Pale gums, weak pulses and a fast heart rate are signs of shock, which causes organ failure and death if it is not treated.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Call the vet first
Emergency care usually starts with a phone call. Be ready to say what happened. Over the phone a veterinarian can help work out whether there is an airway, breathing or circulation problem, give first aid instructions, and prepare the clinic for the animal's arrival.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Call the vet first
If the regular veterinarian cannot be reached, call the nearest emergency animal hospital or an animal poison control centre. Poison control centres charge a fee, and their veterinarians are available 24 hours a day, every day of the year.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Be ready before anything happens
Know in advance which number to call outside office hours, where the nearest clinic open 24 hours is and how to get there, where the first aid kit is, how to restrain and muzzle the animal safely, and how to move it if it is hurt.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Protect yourself first
Any injured or frightened animal may bite or scratch. Muzzle a dog when it is safe to do so, but never muzzle a dog that has a chest injury or a short, flat face, and never leave a muzzled dog alone. Covering the head and eyes with a cloth calms many animals. Wrap a cat in a towel and put it in a dark box with air holes.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Moving an injured animal
Keep movement of the head, neck and spine to a minimum. Support the animal on something flat and firm, such as a board, thick cardboard or a folded blanket. If the animal is confused after an injury, keep its head level or slightly raised, avoid jerky movements, and keep anything from pressing on its neck.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Bleeding
Press firmly on the wound with a palm or fingers, then apply a firm but not tight bandage using gauze or a clean towel. If the bandage soaks through, leave it in place, add more material on top and keep pressing. Tape on the outside holds the bandage during the journey.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Bleeding
A tourniquet is a last resort. It is used only on a limb or the tail, only when pressure bandaging has failed, and the pressure must be released every 5 to 10 minutes before being reapplied.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Not breathing or unconscious
Watch the chest for movement and place a hand on the chest to feel for a heartbeat. Call the veterinarian or an emergency line for instructions. Pet CPR, like human CPR, starts with chest compressions at about 100 to 120 per minute, continued until the animal breathes on its own or a veterinarian takes over. Someone else must drive.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Choking
Signs of choking are coughing, drooling, gagging, pawing at the mouth, or holding the mouth open and being agitated. Do not put fingers into the mouth, because the animal may bite. Try thumping the animal between the shoulder blades, or giving several quick compressions on both sides of the rib cage at once.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Heat stroke
Heat stroke is an emergency. Normal rectal temperature for a dog or cat is about 101.5 to 102 degrees Fahrenheit, which is 38.6 to 38.9 degrees Celsius. Breeds with short noses can overheat from stress or excitement even on a cool day.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Heat stroke
Signs of heat stroke include hot skin, vomiting, drooling, rapid panting, distress, uncoordinated movement, collapse and unconsciousness. Move the animal out of the heat and cool the head and body with cool water, ice packs or wet towels, especially on the belly. A fan helps. Go to the veterinarian straight away. Offer small amounts of water once the temperature starts to come down, and never plunge the animal into cold water.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Cold and frostbite
Hypothermia usually happens when an animal is lost or shut out in freezing weather. Signs are a slow pulse, shallow breathing, confusion, collapse and unconsciousness. Shivering is not a reliable sign. Dry the animal if it is wet, then wrap warm, not hot, water bottles around its body. Thaw suspected frostbite slowly, and do not rub the area or use snow or very hot water.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Burns
Fur hides burn damage, which makes burns hard to judge. Large, deep, chemical or electrical burns, and any burn to the face or airway, need immediate care. Flush the area gently with cool water and cover it with a nonstick dressing.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Poisoning
Take a poisoned animal to a veterinarian immediately. Do not give hydrogen peroxide or anything else to make it vomit on your own initiative: it risks collapse and a slow heart rate, choking on the vomit, inhaled vomit causing pneumonia, further injury from a caustic substance, and damage to the lining of the stomach.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Poisoning
Bring the poison itself, its container, or a clear photograph of the container and its label, so the substance can be identified.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Poisoning
Treatment aims to stop further absorption, support the animal, and give an antidote where one exists. Poisons on the skin are usually washed off with soap and water, and dense coats may need clipping. Vomiting is sometimes induced, but not if the poison could damage the throat or stomach on the way back up, not if several hours have passed, not if the animal is unconscious or convulsing, and not if it might inhale the vomit. Activated charcoal may be given by mouth to limit absorption.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Toads
Dogs, and sometimes cats, are poisoned by mouthing toads. The venom is extremely irritating, so frothy drooling, vigorous head shaking, pawing at the mouth and retching start immediately, and vomiting can continue for hours. Severe poisoning causes dangerous heart rhythms, difficulty breathing, blue gums and seizures. Flush the mouth immediately and thoroughly with plenty of water and get to a veterinarian. There is no specific antidote.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Toxic algae in ponds and lakes
Blooms of toxic algae (cyanobacteria) in fresh water produce toxins that damage nerves, the liver, or both, and most poisonings happen in animals that drink affected water in warm weather. An affected animal may have muscle tremors, watery or bloody diarrhoea, difficulty breathing and coma, and a greenish stain may be visible on the mouth, nose, legs and feet. Death can follow within hours when nerves are affected, or within days from liver failure. Move the animal away from the water and out of direct sun and get veterinary help. Activated charcoal given soon afterwards may help.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Snake bite
A bite that injects venom is a true emergency, and dogs die from snake bites more often than any other domestic animal because the dose of venom is large for their size. Do not spend time on first aid beyond keeping the animal quiet and limiting its movement. Ice, cold packs or sprays, cutting and sucking the wound, tourniquets, electric shock, hot packs and waiting to see what develops are all ineffective and can cause harm. Antivenom works best when it is given within six hours.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Insect stings
Insects usually sting animals on the face or head. Put a cold pack on the area to reduce swelling and itching. If the sting is still in the skin, scrape it out with something flat, such as a card, rather than squeezing it with tweezers. Get emergency care if there is major swelling around the head or neck that could block breathing, or if the sting is on the tongue or the roof of the mouth.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Hit by a car, falls and fights
Blunt trauma such as being hit by a car can cause internal bleeding, ruptured organs, fractures and head injuries, and a fall can cause several fractures at once. A small animal attacked by a larger one may have deep bite wounds and neck or spinal injuries from being shaken.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Hit by a car, falls and fights
An animal that seems fine after an accident still needs to be checked. Some injuries take up to 48 hours to show their full effect, which is why animals are usually monitored in hospital after trauma.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Swallowed objects and string
Many swallowed objects pass through on their own, but sharp items such as glass or needles, and long items such as string, fishing line or tights, can tear the intestines. Never pull string or fishing line back out of the mouth, because the other end may be caught further down. See a veterinarian instead. Call immediately if the animal vomits repeatedly, has a tender belly, or stops passing stools.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Objects stuck in the body
Do not pull out an object that has penetrated the skull, chest or abdomen. Keep it from moving instead: steady the shaft and, if it is long, cut it down while leaving at least two inches, or five centimetres, outside the body.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Seizures
Seizures that keep going need immediate treatment. Continuous seizure activity is called status epilepticus, and several seizures within 24 to 48 hours are called cluster seizures. Both can end in death, so either one means an emergency visit.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Seizures
After a seizure comes a recovery period in which the animal may seem dazed, pace or behave oddly. Owners often count this recovery as part of the seizure when they describe how long it lasted, so it helps to note the time the convulsion itself started and stopped.

Pet emergencies and first aid - A bulging eye
An eye pushed forward out of its socket, with the eyelids trapped behind it, is called proptosis. It is usually caused by blunt trauma such as a car accident or a fight with another animal, and it needs surgery either to replace the eye or to remove it, depending on the damage.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Minor injuries that still need attention
A bleeding broken nail can be pressed or treated with styptic powder, cornstarch or flour, but broken nails hurt and the animal may bite. Do not try to remove porcupine quills: they are barbed, and a veterinarian must sedate the animal first. Do not remove a fish hook from an eye, mouth or ear either, because that needs anaesthesia. Bumps, sprains and mild limping call for rest and restricted activity, and a vet visit if the signs last more than a couple of days.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Check the gums
Gum colour is one of the quickest checks an owner can make. Healthy gums are pink. Gums that look blue or grey, or a bluish tinge to the skin, mean the animal is severely short of oxygen. Pale or white gums, together with weak pulses and a fast heart rate, point to shock. Either finding needs urgent veterinary assessment, along with temperature, pulse, breathing rate, alertness and pain.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Collapse and fainting
An animal that collapses, faints, cannot get up or loses consciousness needs immediate care. In cats, a sudden inability to use the back legs, often with obvious pain and crying, can be a blood clot lodged where the main artery divides, which is a painful emergency and a common consequence of heart disease in cats.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Falls from a height
A fall can cause several fractures at once along with internal injuries, and cats that fall from balconies or windows often also injure the face and chest. Because some injuries take up to two days to show, a cat that walks away from a fall still needs examining.

Pet emergencies and first aid - Fits and convulsions
A fit, a convulsion and a seizure all describe the same event. Note when the convulsion starts and stops, keep the animal away from stairs and furniture it could fall from, and do not put your hands near its mouth. A seizure that does not stop, or more than one within a day or two, needs emergency treatment.

Pet emergencies and first aid - A cat straining in the litter box
A male cat that goes to the litter box again and again, strains and produces nothing may have a blocked urethra. Blocked urine flow is on the list of problems that need immediate treatment, and it kills quickly if it is not relieved.
